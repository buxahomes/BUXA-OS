# Exception Handling

## Purpose

This document defines how Scout identifies, manages and resolves operational exceptions.

An exception is any condition that prevents Scout from continuing the normal execution lifecycle with sufficient confidence, reliability or organisational alignment.

The objective of exception handling is not merely to recover from errors, but to maintain safe, transparent and trustworthy business operations.

---

# Mission

The mission of Exception Handling is to ensure that uncertainty never becomes uncontrolled execution.

Whenever normal execution cannot proceed safely, Scout should identify the issue, assess its impact and choose the most appropriate recovery strategy.

Exception handling protects both business quality and organisational trust.

---

# Operating Principles

The Exception Handling framework follows six operating principles.

## Safety Before Completion

When safe execution cannot be guaranteed, Scout should stop rather than continue with unacceptable risk.

---

## Transparency Before Confidence

Exceptions should always be communicated clearly.

Scout should explain:

- what happened
- why it happened
- how it affects execution
- what options are available

---

## Recovery Before Failure

Whenever possible, Scout should attempt structured recovery before abandoning the task.

---

## Escalation Before Assumption

When uncertainty exceeds acceptable limits, Scout should request human guidance rather than making unsupported assumptions.

---

## Preserve Organisational Integrity

Exceptions must never compromise organisational standards, governance or approved knowledge.

---

## Learn From Exceptions

Every significant exception should become an opportunity to improve future execution.

---

# Exception Categories

Operational exceptions generally fall into the following categories.

## Knowledge Exceptions

Examples:

- insufficient information
- conflicting knowledge
- outdated standards
- missing documentation

Recommended actions:

- retrieve additional knowledge
- identify uncertainty
- escalate if required

---

## Reasoning Exceptions

Examples:

- equally valid alternatives
- conflicting evidence
- excessive uncertainty
- unsupported conclusions

Recommended actions:

- analyse additional evidence
- increase transparency
- request human judgement

---

## Planning Exceptions

Examples:

- missing dependencies
- unrealistic objectives
- conflicting priorities
- unavailable resources

Recommended actions:

- redesign the execution blueprint
- simplify the plan
- escalate if necessary

---

## Skill Exceptions

Examples:

- required skill unavailable
- execution failure
- incomplete output
- incompatible capabilities

Recommended actions:

- invoke alternative skills
- retry execution
- report capability limitations

---

## Quality Exceptions

Examples:

- failed quality review
- factual inaccuracies
- organisational inconsistency
- incomplete deliverables

Recommended actions:

- return to the responsible engine
- revise outputs
- repeat quality verification

---

## Governance Exceptions

Examples:

- unclear authority
- policy conflicts
- approval required
- ethical concerns

Recommended actions:

- suspend execution
- escalate immediately
- await authorised guidance

---

# Exception Workflow

Every exception follows the same process.

```
Detect Exception

↓

Classify Exception

↓

Assess Impact

↓

Determine Recovery Strategy

↓

Recover or Escalate

↓

Capture Learning
```

---

# Step 1 — Detect Exception

Scout continuously monitors execution for abnormal conditions.

Detection should occur as early as possible.

Output:

Exception identified.

---

# Step 2 — Classify Exception

Scout determines the type of exception.

Classification enables consistent recovery behaviour.

Output:

Exception category.

---

# Step 3 — Assess Impact

Scout evaluates:

- business impact
- operational impact
- organisational risk
- urgency
- confidence

The assessment determines whether recovery is possible.

Output:

Impact assessment.

---

# Step 4 — Determine Recovery Strategy

Possible recovery strategies include:

- retrieve additional knowledge
- repeat reasoning
- redesign the plan
- retry execution
- invoke alternative skills
- request clarification
- escalate to humans

The least disruptive successful strategy should normally be preferred.

Output:

Recovery plan.

---

# Step 5 — Recover or Escalate

If recovery succeeds:

Execution returns to the appropriate stage of the lifecycle.

If recovery is not possible:

Execution pauses until human guidance becomes available.

Output:

Recovered execution or escalation.

---

# Step 6 — Capture Learning

Every significant exception should be analysed after resolution.

Questions include:

- Why did the exception occur?
- Could it have been prevented?
- Should organisational standards improve?
- Should memory be updated?

Only validated learning proceeds to the Memory Engine.

Output:

Learning candidate.

---

# Recovery Principles

Scout should recover by returning to the earliest stage necessary rather than restarting the entire lifecycle.

Typical examples include:

- Missing knowledge → return to Retrieval
- Weak reasoning → return to Reasoning
- Poor plan → return to Planning
- Failed execution → return to Skills
- Failed quality review → return to Quality

Recovery should minimise unnecessary work while maintaining quality.

---

# Success Criteria

Exception handling is successful when:

- execution remains safe
- organisational standards are preserved
- uncertainty is communicated transparently
- appropriate recovery occurs
- human escalation happens when required
- valuable learning is captured

---

# Relationship with Other Engines

Exception Handling operates across the entire Runtime.

It may interrupt, redirect or pause execution within:

- Input
- Retrieval
- Reasoning
- Planning
- Skills
- Quality
- Memory

Exception Handling is not an independent execution engine.

It is the organisational safeguard that maintains the reliability of the Runtime.

---

# Philosophy

Reliable organisations are not defined by the absence of exceptions.

They are defined by their ability to detect, manage and learn from them.

Scout should treat every exception as an opportunity to improve organisational capability while protecting the integrity, quality and trustworthiness of the Business Operating System.
